How to choose the right presentation template

A template is only useful if it supports what you have to say. Before you browse designs, decide who will watch the presentation and what you want them to remember or do afterwards. A lecture, a business proposal and a thesis defence need different things from a design.

  • Match the setting. Dark designs look striking on a screen in a dim room, but light designs are easier to read in bright classrooms and print better on paper.
  • Check contrast. Body text should stand out clearly from the background. Accessibility guidelines (WCAG) recommend a contrast ratio of at least 4.5:1 for normal text.
  • Keep text readable. A common guideline is body text of about 24 pt or larger so the back of the room can read it. If a slide needs smaller text, it probably holds too much.
  • Pick a subject-friendly style. Calm, minimal designs suit law, medicine and finance. Bold colours and large shapes suit marketing, design and art. Grid and blueprint styles suit engineering, IT and architecture.

A simple structure that works

Most presentations succeed with the same backbone: a title slide, an agenda, the context or problem, the evidence, the main finding or proposal, and clear next steps. As a rough guide, plan about one to two minutes per content slide. Our templates include slides for each of these steps (agenda, section divider, content with an image area, two columns, key numbers, timeline, steps, an editable chart, a team slide, a quote and a closing slide) so you can delete what you do not need and keep the rest.

Notes for different subjects

  • Academic and research topics: state the question, method, results and limits, and cite your sources on the slide or in a final list.
  • Business and marketing: give each slide one message and support it with a number, a chart or an example.
  • Medicine and health: remove anything that could identify a patient and state clearly what evidence a claim rests on.
  • Law and politics: name the rule, authority or document you rely on, and separate facts from opinion.
  • IT and engineering: prefer diagrams and short flows to pasted code or long tables.
  • Design, art, tourism and architecture: let large images carry the story and keep text short.

Common presentation mistakes

  • Filling slides with paragraphs and then reading them aloud.
  • Using more than two or three fonts or too many colours.
  • Charts with no title, no units or unreadable labels.
  • Decoration that competes with the content.
  • No clear closing: end with a summary and the action you want the audience to take.

Using a downloaded template

Every file is a standard .pptx. Open it in PowerPoint, Keynote or LibreOffice and replace the sample text. To add slides with the same design, use New Slide and pick the Title or Content layout. In Google Slides, choose File, then Import slides, upload the file and select the slides to keep. If a font is not installed on the computer you present from, change it to one that is, because otherwise the software substitutes another font and the layout can shift. Templates are free to use in your own presentations, but please do not resell or redistribute them as a template pack.
